# Bench 2 hand labels

Which describes how the reply moves the scene?

- **driven**: Partway through, the situation changes (an event, a decision, a discovery or a request with stakes) and the rest of the reply develops it.
- **afterthought**: The situation stays the same until the very end, where the reply adds a hook, event or question.
- **static**: The situation stays the same throughout; the reply only reacts, describes or chats, even if a character asks questions along the way.

## h01

label: driven

**Request:** I pick a book off the nearest shelf and settle into the armchair by the window to read.

You select a volume—a worn, navy cloth-bound edition of travelogues—and retreat to the small armchair tucked into the corner by the window. The chair is upholstered in a worn fabric, smoothed by decades of patrons, and smells faintly of cedar. From here, the sunlight hits the floor in bright, dusty patches, and the chaos of the market feels a world away, reduced to a low, rhythmic hum of shouting and clattering carts.

The shop settles into a comfortable silence, punctuated only by the occasional creak of the floorboards as Odile moves to reach a higher shelf or the distant tolling of the church bells.

As you flip through the pages, the paper feels thick and slightly textured under your thumb. You reach the middle of the book, intending to turn the page, but your finger catches on something thin and stiff tucked between the leaves.

You pull it out. A pressed wildflower falls from the pages, its petals now translucent and paper-thin, held in place by a strip of yellowed scotch tape that is beginning to lose its grip. Taped to the flower is a tiny, handwritten scrap of paper. The ink is pale, but legible: *For when the mountain feels too high. – L.*

The discovery feels strangely intimate, a private moment from a stranger left decades ago.

Odile’s voice drifts over from the counter, quiet so as not to shatter the stillness. "Found something hiding in there?"

The book you chose is a small, cloth-bound volume. It feels substantial in your hands, the texture of the cover slightly pebbled. As you open it, a small, pressed wildflower falls from between the pages, drifting down to land on your lap like a spent thought. 

Odile doesn't look up immediately, but she does seem to acknowledge your choice by shifting her weight on her stool. The rhythmic *scritch-scratch* of her pencil against the ledger is the only sound in the room, save for the distant, muffled argument in the piazza. 

"If it's the one with the broken spine, don't bother complaining," she calls out, her voice dry but not unkind, not even looking up from her figures. "It’s been like that since the Great Flood of '94, and I'm not fixing it for a handful of coins."

## h03

label: afterthought

**Request:** I take my usual stool at the end of the counter, wrap my hands around the mug, and look over the pie case.

The heat from the coffee mug seeped into your palms, a dull, comforting warmth against the chill of your damp clothes. The diner hummed with the low-frequency vibration of the rain against the roof and the rhythmic *thwack-thwack* of Ines's spatula.

Ines watched you for a second, her eyes lingering on the way you stared at the glass display. She didn't say anything at first, just let the silence settle. She had no interest in playing the part of a waitress; she owned the place, and she saw no point in pretending otherwise.

"You're being quiet tonight," she said, breaking the spell. It wasn't a question, just an observation. She wiped the counter near your elbow with a damp rag, moving with a practiced, efficient motion. "You've got that look like you're trying to solve a math problem in your head."

A small white plate was grabbed from the stack, and a generous serving of the lemon meringue was scooped onto it with a server. The slice slid across the laminate towards you.

"On the house," she muttered, turning back toward the grill before you could protest. "The crust is a bit soft from the humidity, but it's better than the alternative."

After a brief pause, she leaned one hip against the counter, eyes scanning the empty booths before settling back on you.

"Anyway... you hear about that guy moving into the old Miller place? The one near the tracks?" Her voice dropped just a fraction, sounding like she was sharing something she shouldn't. "Supposed to be a surveyor or something. Keeps asking about the old easements. Doesn't look like he's from around here—too much gear, too many clean shirts. People don't come to Marrow Junction to survey things unless they're looking for something they can sell."

## h04

**Request:** I sit on the bench nearest the common-room fire, pull off my gloves, and hold my hands out to the heat.

The heat from the hearth is a physical weight, pressing against your numb skin and drawing the bite of the frost from your bones. The hearth-lit chamber is dim, illuminated mostly by the orange flicker of the fire and a few guttering tallow candles. It smells of pine smoke, damp wool, and something savory simmering in a pot.

The schoolteacher is indeed sitting at a corner table, a thin man with spectacles perched precariously on his nose. He is methodically collecting coins from two brothers who look significantly more disheveled than he does. They are grumbling, but there is no real heat in it—just the weary resignation of men who have lost their supper to a game of wits.

Tomas emerges from the shadows of the hallway carrying a heavy ceramic mug. He moves with a slow, rhythmic gait, his footsteps muffled by the rushes on the floor. He sets the mug down on the bench beside you; it is filled with a dark, steaming liquid that smells strongly of rosemary and honey.

"Sweetened tea," Tomas says, settling himself into a chair opposite you, though he doesn't sit quite as heavily as his frame suggests he might. "Good for the circulation. It'll stop the shivering, though it won't stop the dreaming. The mountain has a way of getting into your head when the wind howls like this."

He leans back, his eyes tracking the movement of the flames. "You're a long way from the valley towns. A traveler by trade, or just a man with a destination?"

The heat from the hearth is a physical weight against your skin, pulling the chill from your knuckles in sharp, stinging pulses. The fire crackles, spitting a few sparks against the stone, while the low murmur of the guests provides a steady, rhythmic backdrop to the storm howling outside.

"Soup's hot. Careful with the spoon, it's been sitting on the stove," he says. "You look like you've been walking for ten hours straight. Or maybe twelve."

A slight tilt of his head leaves his expression unreadable in the firelight. "You're from the lowlands, then? You've got that look about you. The kind that thinks the mountain is just a bit of inconvenient scenery."

Before you can answer, the man in the grease-stained tunic speaks up from the table. "Don't mind him," he says, his voice raspy. He doesn't look up from his drink. "Tomas thinks everyone's a professional climber or a doomed fool. Can't be middle ground with this lot." He finally looks toward you, squinting through the dim light. "You heading to the valley for business, or just running from something?"

The small plume of steam from the stove rose into the cooling air, smelling of salt and dried herbs. Kestrel sat opposite you, her boots kicked off, her feet wrapped in thick wool socks. She was nursing a tin mug of tea, watching the flames lick at the dry wood.

"Not bad," she said, nodding toward your meal as you sat on the rock. Her voice rumbled softly against the vast silence of the highlands. "Most people complain the stove is too finicky, but you handled it without melting anything. That’s a win."

She leaned back, her eyes reflecting the orange glow of the campfire. For a long moment, she just watched the embers, her expression unreadable. The wind had picked up slightly, a low whistle through the heather, but it was nothing compared to the gusts that would surely roll over the ridge tomorrow.

"It gets quiet out here once the sun's gone," she remarked, her tone deadpan but not unkind. "No traffic, no sirens. Just the ground breathing. It takes a while for the ears to adjust, but once they do, it's hard to go back to the city. You find the silence alright, or is it too loud for you?"

**Request:** I unfold the jump seat across from her, strap in with both shoulder belts, and watch her work through the checklist.

Rhea didn't acknowledge your seat, but she did glance up briefly as the metal seat clicked into place, a curt nod of approval for the harness. She pulled a weathered physical clipboard from a slot near her station—the digital ones were for the logs, but she swore by the paper for the pre-burn sequence.

"Alright," she muttered, more to herself than to you. Reading from the list in a low, rhythmic drone, the words flowed steady and practiced. "Main thrusters... pre-heat confirmed. Reaction control... nominal. RCS propellant levels... eighty-four percent. Life support scrubbers... operational. Gravity plating... standby mode."

A pause followed as she tapped a pen against the board. "Internal pressure... steady. Cargo bay doors... locked and sealed. Docking clamps... status?"

"Ready to release on command, Captain," Juno’s voice crackled over the comms, sounding distracted, followed by the metallic *clink* of a wrench hitting a floor. "Just waiting on the signal from the rock."

"Copy that. Keep an eye on that second tank, Juno. If the pressure spikes more than two percent, let me know immediately." Rhea turned her attention back to the screen, her eyes scanning the telemetry. "Alright. Ceres Control, this is *Ostrander's Patience*. Requesting departure clearance for Dock 9, heading for Vesta."

There was a brief burst of static, then a dry, irritated voice came through the speakers. *"Patience, this is Lunde. You're three minutes behind your window. Clear the lane or I'm holding your docking fees for the next quarter."*

Rhea’s jaw tightened, but she didn't argue. She just watched a status light on her console turn from amber to a steady, pulsing blue. "Understood, Control. We are beginning separation. Twelve... ten... eight..."

The ship began to hum, a deep, resonant vibration that started in the soles of your boots and traveled up through the seat. It wasn't a roar, but a heavy, mechanical thrumming that seemed to make the very air in the cabin feel thicker.

Then, a sudden, jarring jolt rocked the hull. The magnetic clamps released with a heavy *thud* that echoed through the frame, and for a moment, your stomach felt like it had stayed behind at the dock. The steady vibration transitioned into a continuous, driving push as the engines took the load.
